Adversarial debiasing (Zhang et al.)

Algorithms AIMech:ZhangAdversarialDebiasing DRAFT
Aliases: Zhang projected-gradient adversarial debiasing
An adversarial learning algorithm that trains a task predictor alongside a protected-attribute adversary, modifying the predictor gradient with a projection term and an opposing adversary-loss term to target a selected statistical fairness condition.
Identity Notes & Concept Scope This record isolates Zhang, Lemoine and Mitchell’s projection-based method. It is not the whole family of adversarial fairness approaches, a generative adversarial network, or generic gradient reversal. Different adversary inputs target different fairness constraints rather than defining synonyms for those criteria. Claims & Grounded Evidence

2 Claims
Mechanism #method-scope
Train the adversary to predict the protected attribute from differentiable predictor outputs, optionally also using true labels for equalized odds or a label-restricted training subset for equal opportunity. For the predictor, remove the component of its task-loss gradient along the adversary-loss gradient and subtract a weighted adversary-loss gradient from the update gradient. The source defines a zero projection when the adversary gradient is zero.
  • supports Source: Mitigating Unwanted Biases with Adversarial Learning · Locator: Section 3 equation (1), PDF pp. 2–3; Section 5 Propositions 1–3 and footnote 4, PDF pp. 3–4; Section 8, PDF p. 6
Limitation #projection-stability-limit
The FR-Train authors explicitly omit the Zhang et al. projection term, reporting that it worsened training stability in their experiments. This is a setting-dependent design observation, not evidence that projection-based adversarial debiasing always fails.
  • supports Source: FR-Train: A Mutual Information-Based Approach to Fair and Robust Training · Locator: Section 3.1, PDF p. 4, paragraph discussing Adversarial Debiasing immediately before Section 3.2

⚠️ Curated Limitations & Negative Results

  • The source’s convergence argument assumes convexity/concavity, differentiability and convergence; it explicitly notes that the convexity/concavity assumptions usually fail for neural networks.
  • Turning adversarial indistinguishability into a population fairness condition requires a sufficiently expressive and well-optimized adversary. Failure of a restricted adversary to detect dependence is not proof of general independence.
  • Training can be unstable and is sensitive to optimization settings and loss weighting; the method does not guarantee convergence or exact finite-sample fairness.
  • The projection describes an ideal gradient construction. This record does not certify every finite-step or adaptive-optimizer implementation, and omits numerical benchmark claims.
