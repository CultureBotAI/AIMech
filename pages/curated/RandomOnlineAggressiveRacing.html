Random Online Aggressive Racing

Algorithms AIMech:RandomOnlineAggressiveRacing DRAFT
Aliases: ROAR, Random Online Adaptive Racing
An algorithm-configuration procedure that proposes uniformly random parameter settings and compares each challenger with an incumbent through adaptive evaluations on shared problem instances and random seeds, without fitting a predictive performance model.
Identity Notes & Concept Scope The 2011 source expands ROAR as Random Online Adaptive Racing in its introduction and Random Online Aggressive Racing in Section3. Both wordings here refer to that source’s same method. It shares SMAC’s intensification procedure but omits SMAC’s random-forest surrogate and acquisition-guided proposals. It differs from fixed-evaluation random search and significance-based elimination in F-Race. Claims & Grounded Evidence

1 Claim
Mechanism #method
Initialize by evaluating the default configuration or a random setting when a default is unavailable. Supply uniformly random configurations to the intensification routine; the nominal model is constant and unused for proposing them. Add an incumbent evaluation, subject to the maximum-run cap, on an instance with the fewest incumbent runs and a random seed. Evaluate a challenger on a subset of the incumbent’s instance/seed pairs and compare their empirical costs on exactly those pairs. Reject a worse challenger; otherwise enlarge its tested subset in doubling batches until it has covered the incumbent’s available runs, then replace the incumbent when the challenger remains at least as good. Continue proposing random challengers within the configuration budget.

⚠️ Curated Limitations & Negative Results

  • Aggressive racing in this source makes empirical decisions without waiting for a statistical significance threshold. An early rejection can discard a configuration with better expected performance; no family-wise error control or finite-budget optimum is claimed.
  • The shared intensification routine caps incumbent runs at maxR=2000. Its time-budget early-exit guard requires i>=2, but ROAR supplies one challenger per call, so the proposal loop ends after that one challenger; the guard is not a mandatory two-challenger minimum for ROAR. The paper’s asymptotic discussion assumes consistent estimation and unbounded evaluation growth in a finite configuration space, which are not automatic properties of the capped practical routine.
  • The original procedure lacks adaptive capping of individual poor runs. Uniform configuration proposals do not mean equal allocation of evaluations to every configuration. No implementation or benchmark outcome was reproduced.
